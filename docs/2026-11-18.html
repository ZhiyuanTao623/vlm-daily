Learning Grounded Reasoning from Human Gaze

2026-11-17 cs.CV Carnegie MellonPrinceton arXiv:2611.23209v1

Gabriel Sarch, Declan Campbell, Nicholas Budny, Kia Ghods, Katerina Fragkiadaki, Jonathan D. Cohen, Brenden M. Lake, Taylor W. Webb

Human visual reasoning is driven by systematic exploration and extraction of task-relevant visual evidence. Human gaze provides a window into this process, making gaze a useful source of guidance for visually-grounded reasoning in vision-language models (VLMs). However, human gaze also includes noise and redundancies that may transfer poorly to VLMs. In this work, we investigate how human gaze data can be effectively leveraged to improve visual reasoning in VLMs, while maintaining alignment with human behavior. We collect 7,109 human gaze trials from a diverse and challenging set of visual reasoning tasks, and train grounded reasoning models with different forms of gaze guidance. Across evaluations in chart understanding, visual search, and counting, we find that using human gaze as a prior for grounded reasoning consistently outperforms supervision using the full gaze sequence. Based on these findings, we introduce GGR (Gaze-Guided Reasoner), which achieves greater human alignment and more reliable visual grounding while matching or outperforming leading grounded-reasoning baselines in accuracy. Reinforcement learning further improves task accuracy, gaze alignment, and grounding quality despite using no gaze supervision. Behavioral analyses show that gaze supervised models exhibit more visual exploration before arriving at an answer. Our results suggest that the value of human gaze lies in guiding spatial exploration and shaping reasoning behavior without requiring complete imitation of human behavior.

Visual Harness Scales General VLMs to Medical Images

2026-11-17 cs.CV NYU arXiv:2611.22815v1

Shaowen Wan, Hanqi Jiang, Zihao Wu, Junhao Chen, Weihang You, Ruiyu Yan, Bowen Chen, Tianming Liu et al.

We show that an appropriate visual harness can substantially improve the performance of general vision-language models (VLMs) across diverse medical imaging tasks. We introduce \ours, a medical visual harness that equips general VLMs with interactive visual tools and systematic diagnostic evidence management. With \ours, general VLMs interactively explore medical images and progressively acquire diagnostic evidence, which is preserved in a lossless memory for subsequent retrieval and use. After submitting an initial answer, the VLM receives an assessment of potential omissions and the reliability of identified findings. This assessment guides further image inspection and measurement, followed by answer revision in an iterative agentic loop until sufficient evidence is obtained. Across 11 tasks and four imaging modalities, \ours improves performance on all tasks with Qwen3.8-27B, with notable gains in spleen measurement (63.3\% to 98.3\%), renal search (57.5\% to 71.2\%), and pulmonary nodule detection (0.0\% to 28.2\%). High-capability API-based VLMs achieve even higher performance with \ours. For example, GPT-6.1 Sol improves pulmonary nodule detection from 10.8\% to 69.2\% with \ours, demonstrating the complementary benefits of stronger model capabilities and an effective medical visual harness. Our results highlight the potential of visual harnesses as a new paradigm for scaling general VLMs to medical imaging tasks.

Know When to Zoom: How Small Is Too Small for Vision-Language Models?

2026-11-17 cs.CV Carnegie Mellon arXiv:2611.22709v1

Siddharth Vohra, Min Xu

Online vision-language models often miss small details in large images, because the service shrinks the image, or rejects it, before the model ever sees it. A car 20 pixels wide in an 8000 by 6000 drone photo reaches Claude about 6 pixels wide, and OpenAI's default rejects the photo outright. We measure how much detail survives for seven models from four vendors, using an eye exam of printed codes and 601 questions about small objects in real photos. The smallest text a model reads half the time grows from 6 pixels or less on a small image to a predicted 10 to 13 pixels on a 7680 by 4320 photo at default settings. Zooming wins much of the detail back. Splitting each photo into up to nine tiles raises accuracy by 11.1 to 14.5 percentage points for Claude and Gemini, and by avoiding rejections it lifts GPT-6.1 Sol from 50.7% to 83.7%. A two-step zoom, in which the model first names the region it needs, matches or beats tiles on Gemini and comes within 2.5 points of them on Claude, with about 40% of their image tokens. When a detail is lost, Claude and Gemini both say they cannot tell about half the time, but they fail differently in the rest: Gemini gives a confident wrong answer in 43% to 48% of these cases, Claude in only 5% to 9%. A check based on detail size, run before the call, misses most of the gain from tiles on Claude and Gemini, but Claude's own reply is a reliable trigger: tiling only after an unsure reply comes within 1.4 points of always tiling with 62% to 70% of the image tokens. We turn these findings into five rules for when an agent should zoom, and release the eye exam, every model answer and code at https://siddvoh.com/know-when-to-zoom.

Motion-Aware Reward Modeling for Instruction-Guided Video Editing

2026-11-17 cs.CV USCVanderbilt arXiv:2611.22495v1

Li Li, Xiangbo Gao, Bo Ni, Samyadeep Basu, Viet Dac Lai, Jing Shi, Seunghyun Yoon, Gang Wu et al.

Reward models for instruction-guided video editing can favor temporal consistency at the expense of motion. We study this failure, termed \emph{motion collapse}, using controlled motion-suppression variants of real edits. On VE-Bench, the evaluated composite reward prefers a motion-suppressed candidate in $97\%$ of cases. Learned video rewards and a VLM judge also struggle with mild suppression, while latent optimization and reward-guided fine-tuning exploit this weakness to increase reward as motion declines. A linear probe of our base reward backbone performs near chance on mild suppression, motivating an explicit inter-frame dynamics input. We propose a motion-aware reward model that combines an RGB frame-difference stream with preference training against motion-suppressed and fabricated-motion negatives. A distillation anchor preserves alignment with human quality judgments. On the VE-Bench motion ladder, our model reduces the rate of selecting motion-suppressed candidates to $0\%$ while achieving a Spearman correlation of $0.57$ with human quality ratings. It also detects unseen suppression variants and preserves motion during reward-guided fine-tuning on two editing pipelines. These results support explicitly modeling motion in rewards for video editing.
