One-Shot Calibration via Model Editing

2026-11-16 cs.LG StanfordUC BerkeleyUniversity of Virginia arXiv:2611.20562v1

Jiaxing Qiu, Luke Hakso, Roxana Daneshjou, Ahmed Alaa, Thomas Hartvigsen

Model editing aims to correct errors in large, pretrained models by replacing model generations with user-provided responses. Existing editors assume a single target answer for each edit. However, many real tasks inherently entail uncertainty, resulting in multiple plausible answers with varying likelihoods in user feedback. Such feedback forms a target distribution to not only correct but also calibrate model generations. We therefore propose SoftEdit, the first model editor to leverage uncertainty in user responses and perform one-shot calibration during editing. To generalize calibration to unseen samples, SoftEdit uses co-plausibility relationships between answers while preserving real distributional shifts, balancing the new distributional generality-locality tradeoff. Across three language models, three vision-language models, and two large datasets, SoftEdit achieves state-of-the-art performance, ultimately showing that uncertainty in user responses enables generalizable calibration of pretrained models beyond single-answer corrections.

LEDGE: Separating Critic and Editor Failures in Text-to-Image Self-Correction

2026-11-16 cs.CV University of Maryland arXiv:2611.20446v1

Vyom Agarwal, Mokshda Gangrade, Samet Ayhan, Giacinto Paolo Saggese

Text-to-image (T2I) self-correction combines a vision-language model (VLM) critic that diagnoses errors and proposes instructions with an image editor that executes them. End-to-end evaluation obscures whether failures arise from incorrect diagnoses or an editor unable to execute valid instructions. We introduce LEDGE (Learning Error Diagnosis in Generation and Editing), a framework that separates these bottlenecks. LEDGE compiles oracle edit instructions from GenEval while holding the generated images and the editor fixed, isolating the editor's correction capability from the critic's diagnostic accuracy. On Stable Diffusion 3.5 Large, oracle instructions raise GenEval from 72.0% to 81.2%, versus 74.1% for the baseline VLM critic. Yet 435 of 644 failed images remain unrepaired, exposing a substantial editor gap. We also use oracle labels from disjoint training prompts to supervise the critic. Oracle supervision raises exact diagnosis from 33.3% (the same VLM with our structured output, untrained) to 70.8%. With the same editor, the resulting critic improves GenEval from 42.2% (uncorrected) to 59.4% on Stable Diffusion 1.5, against 50.9% for the baseline critic, and from 72.0% to 76.0% on Stable Diffusion 3.5 Large, despite training only on Stable Diffusion 1.5. Improvements transfer to unseen object classes, a second editor, and independent T2I-CompBench judges and prompts. A small human evaluation confirms the ranking in estimated net corrections but suggests a smaller gain than detector-based evaluation. LEDGE thus distinguishes two limits of self-correction: knowing what to fix and being able to fix it.

DominoBench: Evaluating Multi-Step Physical Reasoning from Still Images

2026-11-16 cs.CV USC arXiv:2611.20385v1

Emilio Ferrara

Can a vision-language model look at a picture of a chain reaction and tell what will happen? Recent multimodal models describe scenes fluently and solve textbook physics problems, which suggests they should also predict how a scene of ramps, ledges, pendulums, seesaws and dominoes plays out once a ball is pushed. We tested this claim on 30 models from 10 families and reached a clear conclusion: unaided, no model predicts the outcome reliably. We built DominoBench, a benchmark of still images of 2D rigid-body scenes whose outcome a physics simulator computes. It holds 854 items in 427 minimal pairs, in which a change of one to three grid steps in one quantity decides whether one person survives, and every item comes with a four-rung hint ladder ending in the exact calculation that decides the outcome. Unaided, frontier models from Anthropic, Google and OpenAI are right about the person whose fate flips in at most 72% of items and answer both twins of a pair correctly in at most 34%. Pointing at the deciding step helps little, and exact geometry given as text barely helps, yet once the deciding calculation and the quantities it needs, including speeds that a still image cannot show, are written out, the same models reach 82-92% on the deciding person, while small open-weight models stay near chance. Physical prediction from a still image therefore fails at the quantitative step: the strongest models use the deciding quantity when they are given it, but do not produce it on their own. For models that plan or act in the physical world, a fluent account of a scene is thus no evidence of a correct prediction: such models must find and compute the deciding quantity unprompted, and DominoBench tests whether they do.

SAGE-VQA: A Training-Free, Self-Evolving Agentic Framework with Grounded Evidence for Knowledge-Based Visual Question Answering

2026-11-15 cs.CV Stanford arXiv:2611.19933v1

Ruirui Shen, Jianqi Gao, Xiangfeng Luo, Yonggang Zhang, Muran Yu, Barney Haoyun Miao, Hang Yu, Yike Guo

Knowledge-based visual question answering (KB-VQA) aims to combine visual information with external knowledge to answer user questions. Existing methods face two main bottlenecks: visual perception errors can propagate into hallucinations, and generalization across scenarios remains limited. To address these issues, we propose SAGE-VQA (Self-evolving Agent framework with Grounded Evidence), a training-free agentic framework that combines evidence verification with experience evolution. Our key insight is to explore an Evidence-Driven Adaptive Reasoning (EDAR) strategy. It accurately acquires and verifies question-relevant fine-grained visual information. It also extracts and refines reusable experience to support robust reasoning across tasks. Specifically, we design a Co-verified Visual Grounding module that verifies question-relevant visual information and provides grounded evidence for subsequent reasoning. We further introduce a Self-evolving Hierarchical Memory (SHM) mechanism. SHM organizes experiences into declarative and procedural memories based on their nature. These memories can be reused in subsequent reasoning and continually refined through feedback, without updating model parameters. Experimental results show that our method outperforms competing methods based on lightweight open-source vision-language models (VLMs) as well as those based on closed-source VLMs. Moreover, our method can serve as a plug-and-play reasoning framework for existing VLMs without modifying their architectures.

Beyond the Training Cameras: Coverage-Controlled Probes of Viewing Direction in Frozen Vision Models

2026-11-14 cs.CV Columbia arXiv:2611.19642v1

Peize Ding

Vision models are trained on 2D images of a 3D world. Do their hidden features encode the direction from which the camera views a scene, and if so, in what form? We study the relative viewing direction $α$ of two objects in rendered scenes where both object centres stay at fixed pixels while the camera moves, so that perspective, shading and shadows change but the projected layout does not. In eight pretrained models, from diffusion models to self-supervised and image-text encoders, $α$ is encoded in a strikingly simple form: a single two-dimensional plane in the hidden space, shared across scenes, in which the difference between the two objects' features traces $(\cosα, \sinα)$. The plane keeps its orientation when lighting, colours, floors and object shapes change. This encoding is more than a summary of a few visible measurements: readouts fitted on one set of camera paths stay accurate at camera positions outside their range in seven of the eight models, whereas decoders of the measured object and shadow geometry, equally accurate within that range, fail there. In the diffusion transformer PixArt-$α$, the plane forms abruptly at a single block and carries direction information to later blocks, yet editing it changes the objects rather than the generated viewpoint. We also examine how this encoding carries over to scanned real objects.

LogT: Logically Think with Images for Visual Search

2026-11-14 cs.LG University of Maryland arXiv:2611.19200v1

Yanjun Fu, Quanzeng You, Jiadong Guo, Yujie Lu, Sai Charitha Akula, Haiping Wu, Miao Liu, Sanghamitra Dutta et al.

Thinking with images empowers vision-language models (VLMs) to use an external Zoom-in tool for fine-grained visual search. Recent works employ supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) with tool-specific designs to enable such capabilities. However, achieving logical reasoning alongside accurate and efficient tool use remains a significant challenge. To address this, we shift the focus away from increasing RL complexity and instead identify SFT data quality as a key bottleneck. We then propose LogT (short for Logically Think with Images), a fully automated SFT data engine that explicitly enforces logic-chain-guided reasoning. LogT generates an SFT dataset featuring challenging, visually grounded questions and structured tool-use traces with human-like logic. We validate our approach by fine-tuning two base models, Qwen2.5-VL-7B and InternVL3.5-8B, on the dataset and evaluating them across seven benchmark test sets. We show that LogT-SFT based on Qwen2.5-VL-7B outperforms the state-of-the-art trained with both SFT and RL, despite using 3.1$\times$ less data and $>120\times$ fewer GPU hours. Furthermore, applying a simple RL recipe without tool-specific design on LogT-SFT yields a model that surpasses prior methods with complex RL setups by a substantial 9.59-point margin. In addition to strong performance gains, our model exhibits higher tool-use accuracy and efficiency, as well as more structured and logically consistent reasoning traces, directly validating the effectiveness of our approach.

RAVEN-Bench: A Paired EO-IR Video QA Benchmark for Aerial Multimodal Understanding

2026-11-13 cs.CV Ohio State arXiv:2611.18880v1

Yu Hu, Jianyang Gu, Yue Cao, Hao Liu, Kangnan Wang, Jozsef Hamari, Zheng Liu, Mohsen Zardadi

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have advanced rapidly in image and video understanding, yet their ability to reason over paired aerial electro-optical and infrared (EO-IR) videos remains poorly understood. Such video poses a harder test than standard RGB video QA: targets are often small, evidence may appear only briefly, viewpoints change with the platform, and useful cues may appear in only one stream. We introduce RAVEN-Bench (Reasoning over Aerial Videos with EO-IR aligNment), a paired EO-IR video QA benchmark for low-altitude aerial multimodal understanding. RAVEN-Bench contains 100 temporally aligned EO-IR video pairs and 1,200 human-authored four-way multiple-choice questions, each independently audited for answer keys, modality requirements, evidence intervals, and group validity. It supports EO-only, IR-only, and paired EO+IR evaluation, and uses hierarchical capability levels and structured question groups to diagnose paired-input gain beyond the stronger unimodal condition, unimodal reliance, target consistency, and evidence-chain coherence. We evaluate frontier, open-weight, and remote-sensing MLLMs, showing that aggregate accuracy can overstate evidence-supported EO-IR reasoning. RAVEN-Bench provides controlled modality conditions and group-level diagnostics for auditing whether progress on RGB video benchmarks transfers to aerial cross-spectral understanding. Code and data are available at https://github.com/yuhu990424/RAVEN-Bench and https://huggingface.co/datasets/TerraSenseAnalytics/RAVEN-Bench.
